# The Tonkva

*The level people: moth-like engineers of a cold world, who built the gates, the ledger and the Engine, and made fairness so automatic that when the machine broke they had forgotten how to do it themselves.* Dead about 1,160 years; what's left of them are the [Hoowae](peoples.md#the-hoowae). Their history in dates: [history](history.md). Their tongue: [languages](languages.md#tonkvan-the-old-tongue). Overview: [lore](README.md). How it's told: [tone](tone.md).

## What they were, and what they couldn't admit

The Tonkva were few, slow, long-lived and cold. Their world, *\*Āltʰa*, "hearth" (now Glacis), was ice but for one belt of volcanic valleys along its equator, and barely warm enough for them there; and everything they built began as a way of sharing warmth fairly. They felt the lanes with their antennae long before they could use them, and when they learnt to build gates they did what they'd always done with heat and food: they counted it, and kept it even. Their name for themselves, *\*Tʰōŋkʷa*, means "the level people" and "the agreed people", because in their tongue those are one word ([mind and matter](consensus.md)).

They weren't wise elders. They over-traded and tore three lanes before anyone else in the Spur had a gate. And what they couldn't admit, even to themselves, was what the Engine did to them: it kept their agreement *for* them. For a thousand years no Tonkva had to agree with another about anything that crossed; the machine did it. When the Engine broke, they found they no longer knew how. The outposts couldn't level a single lane by hand, the gates locked as their own law said they must, and the level people died of being kept.

## History

- **~2,600 BP. The Hearth Count.** Before the gates, the ledger: a counting of every house's warmth and every store's food through the long winters, so nobody froze while a neighbour was warm. The first gate (a grown ring on the nearest lane) inherited it.
- **~2,500–2,250 BP. The Three Tearings.** Trade boomed, and the Tonkva crossed faster than their lanes could agree. Three lanes tore. Whole outposts were lost on the far side of them. The Hoowae still remember the three by name, in a lament with no tune.
- **~2,300 BP. The Lock.** The engineer **\*Sāŋ-tāl** ("ring-holder") wrote the rule every Tonkva gate was built to: a gate that cannot report its count to the ledger **shuts itself**. Better shut than torn. It saved the Reach's lanes for a thousand years, and killed her people's outposts in the Hush.
- **~2,200 BP. The Engine.** **\*Mōr-tāl** ("grows-and-keeps"), a grower of the white mould, raised the Engine (*\*Tʰoŋ-tāl*, "the level-keeper") under the capital, fed by the planet's heat. Every gate reported to it, and it ran the heat-taps that warmed the valley belt under roofs of grown glass, wider and warmer than the springs alone ever could. It was the Hearth Count made into a machine, and the Tonkva loved it the way you love a fire.
- **~1,700–1,300 BP. The Kept Age.** Outposts, not colonies; trade with the Vhossk and through the Throat with the Spur; Oru-Tal seeded as a preserve, Morang as a grow-moon, Cinder planted with fruiting trees; ledger-beacons raised across the Reach. The richest, quietest centuries the Reach has had.
- **~1,160 BP. The Breaking.** An eruption tore through the Engine's heat-taps. The last lane-reader of the capital, **Īla**, had felt the ground's lean for a season and told the Counting Houses; they asked the Engine, whose own count of its heat-taps said all was sound. The Engine stopped, every gate shut by \*Sāŋ-tāl's rule, the hearth froze, and the capital's people died in the first winters.
- **After.** Stranded on outposts never meant to stand alone, the Tonkva dwindled over a few generations into the Hoowae: wanderers who kept the lane-sense and the old words, but not the craft.

## How they lived

- **Warmth was shared, and counted.** A Tonkva house's first duty was its hearth-share; its first shame, to be warmer than its neighbours. They gathered round heat the way moths gather at a lamp, and their word for a friend was "one who shares the warmth".
- **Antennae, not eyes.** They felt lanes, leans and each other's moods through their long feathered antennae, and spoke as much with them as with their voices. A Tonkva crowd was a field of slow-moving feathers.
- **Long lives, few children.** Four or five hundred years, two or three children in a life. They spread as small outposts of a few families, never cities, and expected every outpost to last forever.
- **Accounts as poetry.** What survives of their writing is almost all ledgers: beacon tones, terrace inscriptions, gate counts. To them a well-kept account was beautiful, and a balanced one was a kind of peace.

## People

- **\*Sāŋ-tāl**, who wrote the Lock. Her rule is still in every lantern's dead heart. The Hoowae honour her and can't forgive her.
- **\*Mōr-tāl**, who grew the Engine. The Engine's first entry, under every one since, is the grower's name.
- **Īla**, the last lane-reader of the capital, who felt it coming and wasn't believed because the ledger said otherwise. Every Hoowae reader since has known her story, and [Old Mother Seffa](../npcs/old-mother-seffa.md) has been living it for three hundred years: telling the Reach it leans, to people who trust their books more than her.

## What's left

The Engine, still keeping their accounts; their gates, forced open and leaning; their beacons, reading out the ledger again; their mould, wild on Mirefall and in every core in the Spur; their trees on Cinder, still fruiting; their preserve on Oru-Tal; and a few thousand Hoowae, who feel all of it and can repair none of it. **The truth (for us):** there are no Tonkva left anywhere, sleeping or hiding ([the Waking](the-waking.md#the-truth-for-us-not-the-player)). The Engine is a record of their agreement, not them. That's the sadder story, and the better one.
